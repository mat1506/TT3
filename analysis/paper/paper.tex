  \begin{abstract}
  Paleohydrological reconstructions from the Chilean Altiplano document
  abrupt moisture fluctuations during the last millennia. Although the
  end of the mid Holocene aridity and the onset of more humid conditions
  between 6--4 ka has been identified in numerous regional marine and
  terrestrial sites, the timing of late Holocene dry and humid phases
  shows large regional variability. Laguna Miscanti and Laguna Miñiques
  (23\(^\circ\) 43'S -- 67\(^\circ\) 46'W, 4140 m asl) are
  topographically closed, but connected by surface outflow from
  Miscanti. Sedimentological and geochemical indicators from two new
  cores show large facies changes, i.e.~higher carbonate and evaporite
  deposition during more arid periods and increased organic productivity
  (both algal and macrophyte) during more humid phases. As in most
  Andean lakes located in volcanic settings, large 14C reservoir effects
  occur complicating 14C dating, so the age models include 210Pb and
  U/Th dating. In spite of dating uncertainties, both lakes show similar
  patterns during the last millennium. A humid phase in Laguna Miscanti
  prior to ca 1200 CE is coherent with rodent middens and
  geomorphological features indicative of a major pluvial/recharge event
  at lower altitudes (Atacama Desert) during the Medieval Climate
  Anomaly (ca 800 - 1300 CE). The LIA (1300 -- 1850 CE) is characterized
  by several arid/humid cycles and the last century by a productivity
  increase. The hydrological changes observed during the last millennium
  illustrate the complex dynamics of recent climate evolution over the
  high altitude Andean plateau. Discrepancies between the northern and
  southern Altiplano records and with intermediate latitudes (Central
  Chile) records may reflect contrasting responses to external forcing
  (Westerlies versus South American Monsoon dynamics) along different
  climatic zones.
  \end{abstract}

\hypertarget{materials-and-methods}{%
\section{Materials and methods}\label{materials-and-methods}}

In November 2019, we recovered a sedimentary sequence of 15 cm length,
10 cm width and 280 cm depth of the profile exposed on the western wall
of a 1x2 m excavation in the ALTT (Tagua-Tagua 3, Figure 1). Sediment
samples (5 to 10 g of material) were obtained at 1-cm intervals.
Sixty-six samples were sent to the laboratories of Pyrenees Ecology
Institute (IPE-CSIC) and the Andalusian Institute Earth Sciences
Institute (IACT-CSIC) in Spain for geochemical, pollen and isotopic
analysis. Another set of samples were sent to the Archaeobotany and
Palaeoecology Laboratory of the University of Exeter for the processing
and extraction of diatom and phytolith. Sedimentary facies were defined
and characterized based on macroscopic description (color, bedding
features, grain size, lithology and sedimentary textures) and a machine
learning workflow using the scikit-learn library (cita) in Python.
Sedimentary facies were defined using a dimensionality reduction
(Exploratory Factor Analysis) and unsupervised machine learning (K-Means
algorithm cluster) with three different dataset (elemental geochemistry,
grain size and diatoms)(Hall., 2016).

Samples were taken every 2 cm for analysed of total carbon (TC), total
inorganic carbon (TIC), total organic carbon (\(TOC = T-TIC\)), total
nitrogen (TN) and total sulfur (TS) in a LECO SCN furnace (IPE--CSIC).
The grain-size frequency distributions (between 0.02 um and 2000 um)
were measured in triplicate using a Malvern Mastersizer 2000 APA2000
grain-size analyzer with Hydro 2000G AWA2000. To determine the
grain--size, the samples were pre-treated with 10\% H2O2 and 10\% HCl to
remove organic matter and carbonates, respectively. Robust End-member
mixing analysis (EMMA) was performed using the generic deterministic
EMMA protocol with the R package EMMAgeo v0.9.8. (Dietze et al 2012;
Dietze and Dietze 2019) to infer the sediment sources, transport and
depositional processes. Stable carbon and oxygen isotope analyses on
calcite were carried out every 2 cm by mass spectrometry in bulk
sediment samples following standard procedures and using an IRMS
Finnigan MAT 251. The isotopic values are reported in the conventional
delta notation relative to the PDB standard.

Eleven pollen samples were processed following Faegri and Iversen (1989)
methods to extract pollen grains. Lycopodium tablets were added for
calculating pollen concentration (grains/cm3; Stockmarr(1971)) and
accumulation rates (grains/cm2/yr). Pollen grains were mounted on glass
slides and identified and quantified under an Axiostar Carl Zeiss
microscope (400x and 1000x) using published pollen atlas Heusser (1971)
and the reference pollen collection of the Paleoecology Laboratory at
IPE-CSIC. Pollen counts include 300 terrestrial pollen grains excluding
paludal taxa. Phytoliths were obtained from twenty four samples of
approximately 2 gr of sediment. We followed the standard procedures for
phytoliths extraction (Piperno, 1988, 1994, 2006) using 36\% HCl and
70\% HNO3 to remove carbonates and organic matter respectively.
Phytoliths were separated by heavy liquid flotation using ZnBr2 prepared
to a density of 2.3 g/cm3 (Dickau et al., 2013). Finally, phytoliths
were counted until obtaining a total of 200 per sample and they were
classified according to the International Code for Phytolith
Nomenclature (Madella et al.~2005). Diatoms were extracted
simultaneously with phytoliths. Digested aliquotes were mounted onto
permanent slides with Naphrax. Diatom valves were visualized under a
XXXXX microscope, with an oil immersion objective of 1000x. A minimum of
400 valves were counted for each level, with the exception of the base
levels X, Y, Z, where valves were scarce. Diatoms were identified to
species level with the LM images (1000x) taken using a digital SLR
camera (Canon EOS Rebel) attached to microscope AND SEM???. The
following literature was used for taxonomy and autoecology of the
encountered valves.

The TT3 chronology was reconstructed with thirteen AMS-radiocarbon dates
on charcoal and bulk sediment determined at DirectAMS laboratories, USA.
Results are presented in units of percent modern carbon (pMC) and the
uncalibrated radiocarbon age before present (relative to 1950 CE).
Radiocarbon ages were calibrated using the Southern Hemisphere
calibration curve (SHCal20, Hogg et al 2020). We used the package rbacon
v2.2 (Blaauw and Christen, 2011) for age--depth modeling and to
establish the deposition rates along the sequence. A change point
analysis was carried out to identify significant breaks in the time
series (Bai and Perron, 2003; Killick and Eckley, 2014). Stratigraphic
diagrams were analyzed and plotted using `tidypaleo' v0.1.1, `ggplot'
v3.3.5 and `rioja' v0.9-26 packets in R 4.1.0 software.
